# What is an MVP? Meaning, examples and how to build one

*Updated on 2026-11-13*

An MVP, or **minimum viable product**, is the smallest version of a product that lets you learn whether people actually want it. It is not a cheap or broken version of the final product. It is an experiment that happens to be usable.

This guide covers the definition, how an MVP differs from a prototype, the most common types, a step-by-step way to build one and how to tell whether it worked. It is written by DC Solutions, a small studio that builds and runs its own products.

## What is an MVP?

An MVP is the version of a new product that lets a team collect the most validated learning about customers with the least effort. That is Eric Ries's 2009 definition. The goal is learning, not shipping less: you build just enough to test whether your main assumption about users is true, then decide what to build next.

The definition comes from Ries's own post, [Minimum Viable Product: a guide (2009)](http://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/08/minimum-viable-product-guide.html), where he adds a warning: "MVP, despite the name, is not about creating minimal products." According to [Wikipedia's article on the minimum viable product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_viable_product), the term was coined in 2001 by Frank Robinson and popularized by Steve Blank and Eric Ries, as part of what became the lean startup method.

Three words carry the idea:

- **Minimum:** only what is needed to test one assumption.
- **Viable:** real people can use it and get real value.
- **Product:** something users interact with, not a slide deck.

## What does MVP stand for in software?

In software, MVP stands for minimum viable product: the first release of an app or service with just the core feature that solves one problem for one group of users. It ships to real users so the team can measure how they behave, instead of guessing from opinions and feature wish lists.

In app development, that usually means:

- **one platform** first (web or one mobile platform), not all of them;
- **one core flow** that works end to end, such as sign up, do the main task, see the result;
- **manual work behind the scenes** where automation can wait;
- **basic analytics** from day one, so you can see what people actually do.

What it does not mean: skipping security, privacy or basic quality. A product that loses data or breaks on sign up teaches you nothing about demand.

## What is the difference between an MVP and a prototype?

A prototype tests whether an idea can be built or how it should look, and it is usually shown to a few people. An MVP tests whether people want the product, and it is used by real users in real conditions. A prototype answers "can we?" and "how should it work?"; an MVP answers "should we keep going?"

| | Prototype | MVP | Full product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main question | Can it work? How should it look? | Do people want it? | How do we grow it? |
| Who uses it | The team, a few testers | Real early users | Everyone in the market |
| Built to last | No, often thrown away | Partly, refined or rebuilt | Yes |
| What you measure | Feasibility, usability | Usage, retention, willingness to pay | Growth, revenue, quality |

A **proof of concept** sits even earlier: it checks that one technical piece works at all, often without any interface.

## What are examples of an MVP?

Common MVP types are a landing page that explains the product and measures sign-ups, a concierge MVP where the team delivers the service by hand, a "Wizard of Oz" MVP that looks automated but is run manually behind the scenes, and a single-feature product that does one job well. Each tests demand before a full build.

- **Landing page MVP.** A page describes the product and asks for an email or a sign-up. You measure how many visitors care enough to act.
- **Concierge MVP.** You deliver the result personally to a handful of users. You learn exactly what they need before writing much code.
- **Wizard of Oz MVP.** Users see a normal product; behind it, people do the work that will later be automated.
- **Single-feature MVP.** One feature, built properly, for one audience. Many successful products started this narrow.
- **Closed beta.** A working product opened to a limited group, so feedback arrives before the public launch.

## How do you build an MVP?

Start by writing down the one assumption that would sink the idea if it were false, then build the smallest thing real users can try that tests it. Decide in advance what result would count as success, launch to a small group, measure what they do and choose whether to continue, change direction or stop.

1. **Name the riskiest assumption.** For example: "People preparing for jobs abroad will paste their LinkedIn profile into a tool to get feedback."
2. **Pick one user and one problem.** A narrow audience gives clearer signals.
3. **Cut features until it hurts.** Keep only what is needed for the core flow.
4. **Define success before launch.** A number you will check, such as how many users finish the main task or come back the next week.
5. **Launch small.** A closed beta or a small audience is enough.
6. **Talk to users and read the data.** Behavior beats compliments.
7. **Decide.** Keep building, change direction (a pivot) or stop.

## How do you know if an MVP worked?

An MVP worked if it answered the question it was built to test, even when the answer is no. Look at behavior, not compliments: people finishing the main task, coming back without reminders, recommending it or paying. A clear "no one wants this" found early is a successful MVP, because it saves months of building.

Signals worth tracking:

- **Activation:** share of new users who complete the core task once.
- **Retention:** share who come back after a day, a week, a month.
- **Referral:** users bringing in other users without being asked.
- **Willingness to pay:** pre-orders, upgrades or a clear yes when you ask.
- **Qualitative pull:** users asking for the next feature, not only reporting bugs.

Set the thresholds yourself before launch. There is no universal "good" number; it depends on the product and the market.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who coined the term MVP?
The term was coined in 2001 by Frank Robinson and later popularized by Steve Blank and Eric Ries, whose 2009 definition is the one most people quote today.

### Is an MVP a finished product?
No. An MVP is a working first version built to learn. It should be usable and reliable, but it leaves out everything that is not needed to test the main assumption.

### What is the difference between MVP and MLP?
MLP means "minimum lovable product". It is a variation of the idea that asks the first version to be delightful, not just usable, so early users stay long enough to give feedback.

### Do you need to code an MVP?
Not always. A landing page, a concierge service or a no-code tool can test demand before any custom code. Code becomes necessary when the product's value depends on it.

### How long does it take to build an MVP?
It depends on the product, but the aim is to learn as early as possible. If the first version keeps growing for months before anyone uses it, the scope is probably too large.
